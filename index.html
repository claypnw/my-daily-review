1. Can AI spot breast cancer earlier? NHS launches world's largest trial to find out ▶ Listen

BBC News — November 23, 2026

  • Nearly 700,000 women across the UK are to be invited to join the EDITH trial, at 30 sites, with recruitment already begun at Cambridge University Hospitals; the £10m trial is funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research and led by the University of Warwick, the University of Cambridge and Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust with NHS England.
  • At present two NHS experts look at each mammogram, but in the trial one human reader will be replaced by AI, to test whether AI can improve early detection and ease clinical workload without over-diagnosing.
  • Researchers say smaller studies have shown AI can improve detection, but this trial must check it will not cause harm by recalling women unnecessarily; in one arm AI grades images before NHS experts, in another it reviews images afterwards.

2. Applied Digital signs $5.4 billion AI data center lease with hyperscaler ▶ Listen

Reuters — November 23, 2026

  • Applied Digital said it has leased 150 megawatts of critical IT capacity to an unnamed investment-grade hyperscaler, expected to generate $5.4 billion in contracted revenue over an 18-year base term.

5. San Francisco is enjoying an AI boom, but worries about a crash are growing ▶ Listen

BBC News — November 23, 2026

  • San Francisco is being rejuvenated by AI after a pandemic exodus: OpenAI and Anthropic are based there hiring top talent, rents and house prices are soaring, office vacancies are falling, and local businesses such as dog-walking firms are expanding on AI start-up demand.
  • One study found 60% of all venture capital invested in US AI firms from 2023 to 2025 went to companies in San Francisco; the IMF last month warned a significant AI share-price correction and fall in investment was possible.
  • The city remains about 35,000 jobs short of its pre-pandemic peak, AI firms employ relatively few local workers, and much AI spending goes to data centres outside the Bay Area, so a correction could be very painful mainly through spillover to service jobs, per UC Berkeley's Enrico Moretti.

10. Cutting saturated fat may help offset genetic risk for high LDL-C ▶ Listen

Medical News Today — November 23, 2026

  • Researchers analyzed 20,940 postmenopausal women from the Women's Health Initiative and found the higher a woman's polygenic score for LDL cholesterol, the more dietary saturated fat raised her blood LDL-C; in the top quartile, saturated fat intake was also linked to higher atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk.
  • The researchers also investigated popular ketogenic diets, finding no evident benefit and that they could actually increase LDL-C in people with a genetic predisposition; experts quoted note restricting carbohydrates does not cancel out saturated fat's effect on LDL, though the study did not directly test ketogenic diets.
  • Experts say a low-saturated-fat diet may be especially helpful for people whose genes raise LDL-C risk, and that genetic susceptibility does not mean dietary destiny.
