1. Papa John's turns to AI to check pizzas from customers' photos ▶ Listen

Reuters — November 16, 2026

  • Papa John's is offering a free pizza to customers who send in a photo of their delivered order, as part of an AI-based quality-control campaign to help make sure each pizza is properly cooked and cut.
  • CEO Todd Penegor told Reuters the promotion will help improve pizza quality across franchised restaurants, mirroring in-store cameras that use AI to check that cheese is not burnt, pepperoni is cut smoothly and the crust is shaped well; those cameras stand in only a few hundred of the chain's 6,000 locations worldwide.
  • The promotion began on November 9, runs until November 20, generated thousands of photos in its first few days, and was built in-house with Google Cloud technology; the company reported a 6.5% decline in quarterly same-store sales on November 5.

2. Meet the miso-making engineer behind Anthropic's biggest hit ▶ Listen

CNN — November 15, 2026

  • Boris Cherny, 35, is the creator of Claude Code, Anthropic's wildly popular coding agent that can write code, run commands and access files to accomplish work on a user's behalf, giving Anthropic an early foothold in the workplace.
  • Claude Code surpassed $2.5 billion in run-rate revenue in February, and Anthropic built its general office agent Claude Cowork in 10 days using Claude Code; enterprise AI spending accelerated in late 2025 after Claude Code's release to the general public in May.
  • Cherny said software engineering jobs will start disappearing by the end of this year in their current form, with engineers becoming 'builders' who do much more than coding, while six in 10 tech workers in a Stack Overflow survey of 30,000 programmers said they actively try to avoid using AI.

BBC News — November 16, 2026

  • The head of The Guernsey Institute, Dr Louise Misselke, said students will need to adapt to changing employer demands due to new technology and artificial intelligence, as the institute launched its first survey of employer skills needs in 12 years.

4. AI cheating fears spook South Korea ahead of high-stakes college entrance exam ▶ Listen

Associated Press — November 16, 2026

  • South Korean officials are scrambling to prevent cheating on Thursday's national college entrance exam, taken by more than half a million students, as AI and smart devices make fraud harder to detect.
  • The worries intensified after Han Won-seok, the top scorer on a national tax accountant exam, admitted in a Sunday video to using a concealed camera to photograph questions, sending the images to a generative AI tool and receiving answers on a smartphone.
  • Education authorities will deploy at least one specially trained supervisor at each of 1,300 test sites to identify AI-powered smart glasses, and are considering requiring students to hand over smart devices and shutting down public Wi-Fi at test sites. 5. AI bubble will likely burst and cause a recession by 2028 election, Wall Street's former top cop says ▶ Listen

CNN — November 16, 2026

  • Former SEC chairman Gary Gensler told CNN the artificial intelligence boom is already a bubble that will likely burst and cause a recession before the 2028 presidential election, pointing to an estimated $800 billion a year being plowed into AI infrastructure.
  • Gensler said Big Tech companies are spending five times more in 2026 than three years ago, warned of circular and off-balance-sheet financing, and said neocloud companies are where breakage and bankruptcies are most likely, while major companies will survive.
  • Ed Yardeni countered that the air is coming out of the bubble in a self-correcting way, that there is no irrational exuberance in valuations, and that Gensler's warning is a plausible scenario that helps keep investors grounded.

9. Questions swirl around FAA's new SMART AI system, though officials say no link to ground stop ▶ Listen

Associated Press — November 16, 2026

  • The nationwide airline ground stop since Friday was not related to the FAA's new $875 million AI-powered SMART system, according to government officials and Air Space Intelligence, the company behind the technology, though the timing — SMART expanded nationwide on November 10 for all 22 air traffic control centers, three days before the ground stop — has raised questions.
  • SMART, which stands for Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories, centralizes 200 data streams including weather, flight paths and staffing, and makes recommendations to humans rather than running flight control; in its Washington-area test it helped reroute 180 flights, saving an average of 10 minutes and 80 gallons of fuel per flight.
  • House Democrats asked the Government Accountability Office in October for an audit, raising safety and guardrail concerns, and a former top FAA official said the agency must make sure SMART is just a tool in the toolbox, with ways to verify its information. The headline link goes to the Associated Press story as carried by TechXplore.
