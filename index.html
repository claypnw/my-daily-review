US bank regulator issues first-ever AI guidance for examiners▶ Listen

Reuters — November 4, 2026

  • The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which oversees large national banks, issued what it said was the first-ever guidance for how examiners should probe banks' AI usage, according to a copy of the document seen by Reuters.
  • Under the new framework, banks are not expected to establish separate risk parameters for AI use and examiners should not treat the technology as a unique risk; examiners should focus on the consequences of potential AI-driven mistakes rather than the sophistication of particular models.
  • A simple AI agent authorized to initiate transactions could be considered riskier than a sophisticated model deployed for internal purposes, the guidance says, and higher-risk uses may require greater restrictions on autonomy or more direct human oversight. The document is not legally binding, but banks typically comply closely with such guidance.

How to tell if a voice recording is AI-generated▶ Listen

Scientific American — November 2, 2026

  • A voice can now be cloned from just a few seconds of audio and a couple of clicks: researcher Sarah Barrington of the University of California, Berkeley, clones her own voice from a YouTube clip in a mere 90 seconds in a lecture demonstration, and scammers have already used cloned voices to persuade a large Italian private bank to wire around 105 million dollars abroad.
  • Our ears are bad at telling real from cloned voices: in a study of real and cloned voices of 220 speakers, listeners flagged fakes correctly only 60 percent of the time, and judged a clone and the real speaker to be the same person about 80 percent of the time side by side.
  • Detection now turns on audio forensics — tiny variations in vocal cycles called jitter and shimmer — and on inaudible watermarks some AI audio makers hide in created audio; no single measure shows a recording is fake, so a suite of tools is needed.

China's solar push reshapes traditional ways of life on Tibet's high plateau▶ Listen

Reuters — November 4, 2026

  • Life has become unexpectedly easier and more lucrative for some shepherds on the world's highest plateau after China began carpeting the area with solar panels: Li Yonglin, 25, grazes his sheep freely inside a fenced solar park in Gonghe, Qinghai, and estimates the new approach boosted his earnings to 50,000 to 60,000 yuan (about 7,496 to 8,995 dollars) from 10,000 to 20,000 yuan under the old way.
  • By July this year, state media said, 6,330 households in five townships had benefited from 56 grazing sites in Hainan, a culturally Tibetan area that encompasses Gonghe; the complex Li visits sprawls over 148,000 acres and uses grazing sheep to reduce mowing costs, a project supervisor told Reuters, calling it a win-win, with power sent nationwide to cities such as Beijing and Shanghai.
  • Researchers warn the projects are also eroding traditional ways of life: installations fragment once-open grazing land, plateau dwellers get little say in the conversion of pastures, and solar overtook coal in July as China's biggest source of installed power capacity, with wind and solar generation planned to expand a further 53 percent in the next five years.
